## What is it?

*Nebula* is the Latin word for mist. Astronomers once used it for every fuzzy object in the sky,
galaxies included; today it means a cloud of gas and dust between the stars. Nebulae come in several kinds:

- **Emission nebulae** glow by themselves: stars inside or nearby ionise the gas, and ionised hydrogen
  shines red. Many are star-forming regions.
- **Reflection nebulae** do not shine on their own; their dust and gas are lit by nearby stars and often
  take on the blue colour of those stars.
- **Dark nebulae** are so dense that they absorb light. We see them only as silhouettes against a bright
  nebula or star field.
- **Planetary nebulae** are the glowing outer layers thrown off by a dying Sun-like star. The core left
  behind becomes a [white dwarf](doc:white-dwarf).
- **Supernova remnants** are clouds blown out by exploding stars; they have their own article,
  [supernova remnant](doc:supernova-remnant).

## How do we know?

Star-forming clouds can be huge: NASA describes molecular clouds holding 1,000 to 10 million times the
mass of the Sun and spanning hundreds of light-years. Inside them, dense clumps collapse into new stars.
The **Orion Nebula** (M42) is the closest large star-forming region to Earth, and it is visible to the
naked eye (magnitude 4.0) as a small misty smudge below the three belt stars of Orion. At its centre,
four massive young stars called the Trapezium light up the gas.

Distances to nebulae are often uncertain, because a cloud has no sharp point to measure. In this atlas
they come from parallaxes of objects associated with each nebula, as collected by SIMBAD.

## Surprising but true

- Planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets. William Herschel coined the name because in his
  telescope they looked like hazy round discs, similar to the planet Uranus.
- Near Merope, one of the Pleiades stars, Hubble photographed a reflection nebula that is being
  destroyed by the star's radiation.
- Different sources give different distances. NASA quotes about 1,500 light-years for the Orion Nebula;
  the atlas's catalogue uses 433 pc (about 1,410 light-years). NASA quotes about 2,000 light-years for
  the Ring Nebula; the atlas's catalogue has 787 pc (about 2,570 light-years). Both come from published
  measurements, which shows how hard nebula distances are.

## Where to find it in this atlas

Nebulae from the Messier and NGC catalogues are labelled and searchable. Try:

- [Orion Nebula, M42](#focus=M42), a stellar nursery (433 pc);
- [Lagoon Nebula, M8](#focus=M8) (1.27 kpc) and [Eagle Nebula, M16](#focus=M16) (1.69 kpc), whose
  star-forming region Hubble photographed in 1995 as the "Pillars of Creation";
- [Ring Nebula, M57](#focus=M57) and [Dumbbell Nebula, M27](#focus=M27), planetary nebulae;
- [Helix Nebula, NGC 7293](#focus=Helix%20Nebula), a planetary nebula about 200 pc away.

Some nebulae in the catalogue, such as M78 or M43, have no reliable distance. Searching for them turns
the view towards their direction from the Sun, and the card says "direction only".
