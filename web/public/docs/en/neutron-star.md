## What is it?

When a star of about 8 solar masses or more runs out of fuel, its core can no longer hold itself up.
It collapses in a supernova. In the collapse, protons and electrons are forced together into
neutrons. If the core has roughly 1 to 3 times the mass of the Sun, the neutrons stop the collapse and
what remains is a **neutron star**. A heavier core keeps collapsing into a [black hole](doc:black-hole).

A neutron star is about 20 km across, the size of a city, yet it holds more mass than the Sun. It is
one of the densest objects known: on Earth, a sugar cube of neutron-star material would weigh about
a trillion kilograms, as much as a mountain.

Its magnetic field is typically trillions of times stronger than the Earth's. A special kind, the
**magnetar**, has a field about a thousand times stronger still.

## How do we know?

Most neutron stars we know are [pulsars](doc:pulsar): we detect their regular radio pulses as they
spin. Timing those pulses very precisely tells us their spin, and for a pulsar in a pair of stars, the
orbit gives its mass.

The heaviest well-measured neutron star is the "black widow" pulsar PSR J0952−0607, with
2.35 ± 0.17 solar masses (Romani et al. 2022). There must be a maximum mass above which a neutron star
must collapse to a black hole. That study shows it is at least about 2.2 solar masses, but its exact
value is still an open question: it depends on how matter behaves at extreme densities, which is not
yet fully understood.

On 17 August 2017, gravitational-wave detectors LIGO and Virgo caught the ripples from **two neutron
stars merging**, an event called GW170817, about 40 Mpc away. A gamma-ray burst followed 1.7 seconds
later, and telescopes around the world saw the glow of the explosion. It was the first time one event
was observed in both gravitational waves and light.

## Surprising but true

- PSR J0952−0607 seems to have grown by eating its companion: if it was born at a typical mass, it has
  swallowed nearly one solar mass of gas from the star that orbits it.
- The fastest-spinning neutron star known turns 716 times per second.
- The Crab Nebula's neutron star was born in a supernova recorded on Earth in 1054.

## Where to find it in this atlas

Neutron stars are tiny and faint, so you will not see them as points of light in the map. Their homes
are easier to find. Fly to the [Crab Nebula, M1](#focus=M1) ([article](doc:crab-nebula)), whose
neutron star spins 30 times per second, or search for a pulsar by name (for example "PSR B0833-45",
the Vela pulsar) to look in its direction. For more on the explosions that create them, read
[supernova remnant](doc:supernova-remnant).
