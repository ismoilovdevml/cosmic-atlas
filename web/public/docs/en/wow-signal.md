## What is it?

On the night of 15 August 1977, at about 11:16 pm local time, the Big Ear radio telescope of Ohio State
University recorded an unusually strong, narrow radio signal from the direction of the constellation
Sagittarius. Big Ear was running a search for signals from extraterrestrial civilisations, a field
called [SETI](doc:seti).

A few days later the astronomer Jerry R. Ehman was reading the computer printout. He circled the code
"6EQUJ5" and wrote "Wow!" in the margin. The name stuck.

The signal has never been seen again. Its origin is unknown. **No confirmed signal from an
extraterrestrial civilisation has ever been detected**, and the Wow! signal is not one either: it is an
unexplained event.

## Key numbers

| | Wow! signal |
|---|---|
| Date | 15 August 1977, about 11:16 pm EDT |
| Telescope | Big Ear, Ohio State University |
| Duration | about 72 seconds |
| Peak strength | 30 to 31 times the background noise |
| Frequency (Ehman) | 1,420.4556 ± 0.005 MHz |
| Frequency (2025 re-analysis) | 1,420.726 ± 0.005 MHz |
| Bandwidth | within one 10 kHz channel |
| Direction | Sagittarius, declination about −26°57′ |
| Repeats | none found |

## How do we know?

Big Ear could not be steered across the sky. It looked at one strip of sky while the Earth's rotation
carried the stars through its field of view. A point source in the sky would therefore rise in strength,
peak and fade over about 72 seconds, and that is exactly what the Wow! signal did. This is one reason
it is taken seriously: it behaved like something far out in space, not like nearby interference.

The printout shows the signal strength every 12 seconds as one character: digits for weak values and
letters for stronger ones. "6EQUJ5" rises to "U", meaning 30 to 31 times the noise, and falls again.

The frequency was very close to the hydrogen line at 1,420.4056 MHz, the radio frequency naturally
given off by hydrogen gas. The signal fitted into a single 10 kHz channel, which is very narrow.

Big Ear had two receiving horns pointed at slightly different parts of the sky, and nobody knows which
horn picked up the signal. So there are two possible positions, close to each other.

## Surprising but true

- Big Ear kept watching the same strip of sky for about 60 days afterwards, and the same region was
  scanned many more times in later years. Other observatories searched too. The signal never returned.
- Ehman himself, after ruling out the terrestrial explanations he could think of, said an
  extraterrestrial origin was possible but that the origin remained "an open question".
- **A hypothesis from 2024:** Abel Méndez and colleagues found, in Arecibo Observatory data from 2020,
  narrow signals near the hydrogen line that look like the Wow! signal but are about a hundred times
  weaker. They come from small, cold clouds of hydrogen gas. They propose that the Wow! signal was such a
  cloud suddenly brightening, like a natural maser flare, triggered by a burst of radiation from a
  magnetar (a highly magnetised neutron star). This is a hypothesis, not a confirmed answer.
- In 2025 the same group re-analysed decades of old Ohio SETI records. They found a higher frequency
  (1,420.726 MHz), a peak brightness above 250 jansky and a narrower position, slightly shifted from earlier
  estimates. This may help explain why the source has been so hard to find.
