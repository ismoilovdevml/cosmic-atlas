## What is it?

Most of the radio noise that leaks from Earth into space is accidental. But a few times people have
sent **deliberate messages** meant for other civilisations. Sending such signals is sometimes called
**METI** (Messaging to Extra-Terrestrial Intelligence), the opposite of [SETI](doc:seti), which listens.

The best-known messages:

- **Pioneer plaque (1972).** An aluminium plaque on Pioneer 10 shows a man and a woman, the Solar
  System, and the Sun's position relative to 14 pulsars.
- **Arecibo message (1974).** On 16 November 1974 the 300-metre Arecibo radio telescope in Puerto Rico
  sent 1,679 bits towards the globular cluster M13. 1,679 = 23 × 73, a hint for how to arrange the bits
  into a picture: it shows numbers, DNA, a human figure, the Solar System and the telescope itself.
  The transmission lasted nearly three minutes at 10 bits per second, from a one-million-watt transmitter.
- **Voyager Golden Records (1977).** Each Voyager carries a 12-inch gold-plated copper record with
  115 images, greetings in 55 languages, natural sounds and about 90 minutes of music, with playing
  instructions on the cover. A committee chaired by Carl Sagan chose the contents.
- **Cosmic Call 1 (1999), Teen Age Message (2001), Cosmic Call 2 (2003).** Sent from the 70-metre
  planetary radar near Evpatoria, Crimea, to 4, 6 and 5 Sun-like stars respectively. Cosmic Call 1
  alone was 370,967 bits long.

## How does it work?

A radio message travels at the speed of light, so its travel time in years equals the distance in
light-years. A message to a star 50 light-years away arrives in 50 years; any reply would take another
50.

Spacecraft are far slower. Voyager 1 moves at about 17 km/s relative to the Sun; at that speed it would
need about 75,000 years to cover even the distance to the nearest star.

A message must also be readable by someone who shares no language with us. That is why designers use
mathematics (like the prime factors 23 × 73), pictures, and physical facts such as the positions of
pulsars on the Pioneer plaque.

## Surprising but true

- The Arecibo message was sent during a ceremony marking the telescope's upgrade. It will take about
  25,000 years to reach M13.
- Scientists disagree about METI. Some argue that transmissions so far are faint and very unlikely to be
  detected even near the target stars, and call for international discussion of the risks before
  sending more.
- The Voyager record is meant to be played at 16⅔ revolutions per minute, and its cover shows how to
  play it.
