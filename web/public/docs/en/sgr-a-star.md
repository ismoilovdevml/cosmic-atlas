## What is it?

Sagittarius A* (say "Sagittarius A-star", short Sgr A*) is the
[black hole](doc:black-hole) at the very centre of our galaxy, the [Milky Way](doc:milky-way). It is a
supermassive black hole: it weighs about 4.3 million times as much as the Sun, yet all that mass is
hidden inside a region smaller than the orbit of Mercury.

Sgr A* lies about 27,000 light-years from us, in the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. Thick
clouds of dust between us and the Galactic centre block its visible light, so astronomers study it with
infrared light and radio waves, which pass through the dust.

## Key numbers

| | Sgr A* |
|---|---|
| Mass | (4.297 ± 0.012) million Suns (statistical; about ±0.04 million systematic) |
| Distance | 8,277 ± 9 pc (about 27,000 light-years) |
| Event horizon radius (Schwarzschild radius) | about 12.7 million km (0.085 au), computed from the mass |
| Diameter of the bright ring seen by the EHT | 51.8 ± 2.3 micro-arcseconds |
| Star S2: orbital period | about 16 years |
| Star S2: closest approach | 120 au, at about 7,650 km/s |

## How do we know?

The mass comes from watching stars dance. For about three decades, teams have tracked individual stars
that orbit an invisible point at the Galactic centre, using large telescopes in Chile and Hawaii. The
star S2 completes one orbit in about 16 years. At its closest approach in 2018 it passed only 120 au
from the black hole, moving at about 7,650 km/s, more than 2.5 % of the speed of light. From the size
and speed of such orbits, Kepler's laws give the central mass: about 4.3 million Suns. Since 2016 the
GRAVITY instrument has combined the four large telescopes of ESO's Very Large Telescope into one,
measuring positions to a few tens of micro-arcseconds.

The orbits also test Einstein's general relativity. S2's orbit does not close exactly: it turns by
about 12 arcminutes each loop, just as general relativity predicts, and its light is stretched to
longer wavelengths by gravity when it passes close.

The picture came later. The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) linked eight radio observatories across the
world into one Earth-sized telescope working at a wavelength of 1.3 mm. Its 2017 observations, published
on 12 May 2022, show a bright ring around a dark centre: the shadow of the black hole. The ring is
about 52 micro-arcseconds across, the size a doughnut on the Moon would appear from Earth.

## Surprising but true

- Sgr A* is close to us but was harder to photograph than the much more distant M87*. Gas circles the
  small Sgr A* in minutes, so the image changed while it was being recorded. Around M87* the same takes
  days to weeks.
- M87* is more than a thousand times bigger and heavier than Sgr A*, yet the two rings look about the
  same size on the sky, because M87* is about 2,000 times farther away.
- Any extra mass spread out inside S2's orbit, such as dark matter or many faint stars, can be at most
  about 0.1 % of the black hole's mass.
- Half of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez "for the discovery of a
  supermassive compact object at the centre of our galaxy", the leaders of the two teams that tracked
  these stars.
