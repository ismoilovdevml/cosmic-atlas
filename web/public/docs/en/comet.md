## What is it?

A comet is a small body of ice, dust and rock left over from the birth of the Solar System, about
4.6 billion years ago. NASA calls comets "dirty snowballs": mostly ice coated with dark organic
material. The solid core, the nucleus, is often only a few kilometres across.

Far from the Sun a comet is a dark, frozen lump. When its orbit brings it closer, sunlight warms the
ice, which turns into gas and carries dust with it. This makes a glowing cloud around the nucleus,
the coma, which can stretch for hundreds of thousands of kilometres. Comets grow two tails: a dust
tail and a gas (ion) tail, both pushed away from the Sun.

## Where do they come from?

Short-period comets mostly come from the Kuiper belt, the disc of icy bodies beyond Neptune. Many
long-period comets come from the much more distant Oort cloud, about 100,000 AU from the Sun. A
passing star or the pull of the Galaxy can nudge one of those bodies onto a long fall toward the Sun.

Not every comet belongs to the Sun. Three visitors from other stars have been found so far:
1I/ʻOumuamua in 2017, 2I/Borisov in 2019 and 3I/ATLAS in 2025. Their orbits are hyperbolas: they
pass the Sun once and leave for good. 3I/ATLAS has an eccentricity of about 6.1 in the Minor Planet
Center's elements, far above the value of 1 that separates bound orbits from escaping ones.

## How do we know?

Astronomers measure a comet's position on the sky night after night and fit an orbit to those
measurements. The Minor Planet Center collects the observations and publishes the orbits. Close-up
knowledge comes from spacecraft: ESA's Rosetta orbited comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko from 2014 to
2016 and set down the Philae lander on its surface in November 2014.

## Surprising but true

- A comet's tail does not trail behind it like smoke behind a train. It always points away from the
  Sun, so a comet leaving the Sun travels tail first.
- Halley's comet comes back every 75 to 76 years. It was last near the Sun in 1986; its next
  perihelion, by the Minor Planet Center's orbit, is on 14 August 2061. In late 2026 it is close to
  its farthest point, 35 AU from the Sun.
- 1,744 comets in the Minor Planet Center's list pass within 0.01 AU (1.5 million km) of the Sun's
  centre, and 1,676 of them are named after SOHO, the solar observatory that found them. Most belong
  to the Kreutz group, which ESA describes as fragments of a single comet that broke up. The list
  gives most of them a parabolic orbit (e = 1), so in this atlas they fly off along nearly the same
  path and show up as a thin streak of points tens of AU from the Sun.
