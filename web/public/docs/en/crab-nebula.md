## What is it?

The Crab Nebula, also called M1, is a [supernova remnant](doc:supernova-remnant): the expanding cloud
left behind when a massive star exploded. In the year 1054, Chinese astronomers recorded a "guest
star" in the constellation Taurus. It was bright enough to be seen in the daytime for close to a month.
Today, at that same spot, telescopes show a tangled cloud of glowing filaments: the Crab.

At the centre sits what is left of the star's core: a [neutron star](doc:neutron-star), an extremely
dense, collapsed stellar core. It spins about 30 times every second and sweeps beams
of radiation past the Earth like a lighthouse, so we see it as a [pulsar](doc:pulsar).

The English astronomer John Bevis first saw the nebula in 1731. Its name M1 means it is the first
object in Charles Messier's famous catalogue.

## How do we know?

The date of the explosion comes from history. Medieval Chinese records describe a new star in Taurus in
1054, and the Crab lies where that "guest star" appeared. It is the remnant of that historical
supernova.

The pulsar is timed with radio telescopes. Its pulses arrive so regularly that astronomers can measure
how much the spin slows down: each year, one rotation gets longer by about 13 microseconds. A spinning
neutron star that slows down loses energy. For the Crab that is 4.5 × 10³¹ watts, about a hundred
thousand times the power of the Sun. This energy flows out as a wind of particles and magnetic fields.
Trapped inside the nebula, that wind makes it glow at every wavelength from radio waves to X-rays.

Hubble and the Chandra X-ray Observatory have mapped the structure in fine detail. Hubble images show
bright wisps near the pulsar that move outward at about half the speed of light.

## Surprising but true

- The light of the 1054 supernova had already been travelling for about 6,500 years when it reached
  Earth. The star actually exploded thousands of years before medieval astronomers saw it.
- The pulsar's magnetic field is estimated at about 3.8 × 10⁸ tesla, roughly 10¹³ times stronger
  than Earth's field at the surface (less than 10⁻⁴ T).
- Dividing the spin period by twice its slow-down rate gives a "characteristic age" of about 1,260
  years. The real age, counted from 1054, is about 970 years. The two are close but not equal, because
  the simple formula rests on assumptions about the pulsar's birth spin and braking that are only
  approximately true.
- The outer nebula holds several times the mass of the Sun in material thrown out by the explosion.
