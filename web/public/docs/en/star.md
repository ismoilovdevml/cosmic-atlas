## What is it?

A **star** is a giant ball of hot gas, mostly hydrogen with some helium. Its own gravity squeezes it,
and in the core the pressure and temperature are so high that hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium. That
**nuclear fusion** releases the energy that makes the star shine and keeps it from collapsing.

Our Sun is a typical star. Its radius is 695,700 km, about 109 times the Earth's, and its mass is
about 2 × 10^30 kg. Its surface is 5,772 K. It is roughly halfway through its long, stable
hydrogen-burning life.

Stars are born in cold clouds of gas and dust that can hold 1,000 to 10 million times the mass of the
Sun. Dense clumps collapse under gravity, heat up and become stars.

## How do we know?

Almost everything we know about stars comes from their light.

- **Distance:** for nearby stars, from [parallax](doc:parallax).
- **Brightness:** from photometry, expressed as [magnitudes](doc:magnitude). With the distance, this
  gives the star's true power (luminosity).
- **Temperature:** from colour. Hot stars look blue-white, cool stars orange-red. Gaia measures colour as
  BP − RP: the difference between blue and red magnitudes.
- **Composition and motion:** from spectra, which show the barcode of chemical elements and the
  Doppler shift.

Plotting luminosity against colour for many stars gives the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, the
astronomer's map of stellar life. Most stars, including the Sun, sit on a band called the main
sequence while they burn hydrogen.

## Surprising but true

- A star's mass decides almost everything. Massive stars burn fast and may live only a few million
  years; small red dwarfs burn so slowly they can shine for trillions of years.
- The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is a red dwarf only 12 % of the Sun's mass and about 0.1 % of its
  brightness. It is 1.30 pc (4.25 light-years) away, yet too faint to see without a telescope.
- The universe may hold up to a septillion stars: a 1 followed by 24 zeros (NASA estimate).
- When they run out of fuel, low-mass stars become [white dwarfs](doc:white-dwarf), and massive stars
  explode as supernovae and leave [neutron stars](doc:neutron-star) or [black holes](doc:black-hole).

## Where to find it in this atlas

The [Gaia stars](#layer=stars-gaia) layer shows Gaia DR3 stars within 1 kpc. Each point's colour comes
from its measured BP − RP colour, converted to the colour a body of that temperature would glow. Click a
star to see its magnitude, colour and distance range.

About 650 stars have official IAU names and are labelled. Try [Sirius](#focus=Sirius), the brightest
star in the night sky; [Betelgeuse](#focus=Betelgeuse), a red supergiant; [Polaris](#focus=Polaris),
the Pole Star; or [Proxima Centauri](#focus=Proxima%20Centauri), our nearest neighbour. Stars born
together form [star clusters](doc:star-cluster), such as the [Pleiades](#focus=Pleiades).
