## What is it?

An **exoplanet** is a planet outside our Solar System, usually orbiting another star. For most of
history people could only guess whether other stars had planets. Now we know they are common: NASA
estimates there are billions in our Galaxy, and on 12 November 2026 the NASA Exoplanet Archive counted
**6,470 confirmed** ones.

They come in many kinds. NASA groups them into four families: gas giants, Neptune-like planets,
"super-Earths" and terrestrial (rocky, Earth-like) planets. Many have no match in our Solar System.

## How do we know?

Planets are faint and lost in the glare of their stars, so most are found indirectly.

- **Transit method:** when a planet passes in front of its star, the star dims very slightly.
- **Radial velocity method:** a planet's gravity makes its star wobble towards and away from us. The
  wobble shows up as a tiny back-and-forth Doppler shift in the star's spectrum.
- **Microlensing:** a nearer star passes in front of a more distant one and its gravity briefly
  magnifies the distant star's light; a planet around the nearer star can leave its own short signal.
- **Pulsar timing:** a planet around a [pulsar](doc:pulsar) makes its pulses arrive slightly early or
  late.

## Surprising but true

- Planets were found around a pulsar, PSR B1257+12, as early as 1992, three years before any planet
  of a Sun-like star: a dead star with a planetary system.
- The first planet around a Sun-like star, **51 Pegasi b**, was announced in October 1995. It is a gas
  giant with roughly half to two-thirds of Jupiter's mass (NASA's catalogue page gives both 0.46 and
  0.61) that circles its star in only 4.2 days, at about 0.05 au, much
  closer than Mercury is to the Sun. Its discoverers, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, later shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in
  Physics.
- Our nearest neighbour star, Proxima Centauri, has a planet: **Proxima b** (2016), at least 1.3 times
  the Earth's mass, orbiting every 11.2 days at a distance where water could be liquid on its surface.
- **TRAPPIST-1**, a small cool star 12 parsecs away, has at least seven Earth-sized planets. The six
  inner ones orbit in 1.5 to 12.4 days.

## Where to find it in this atlas

In the atlas, exoplanets are drawn at their host stars, because their orbits are far too small to see
at the scale of the stars. Fly to [51 Pegasi](#focus=Helvetios) (IAU name Helvetios, 15.5 pc) and to
[Proxima Centauri](#focus=Proxima%20Centauri) (1.30 pc, [Proxima b](doc:proxima-b)). TRAPPIST-1 has
its own article: [TRAPPIST-1](doc:trappist-1).

Some planet host stars have official IAU names, like Helvetios for 51 Pegasi. They are in the atlas's
named-star catalogue: search for a name and the info card shows the star.
