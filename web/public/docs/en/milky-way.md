## What is it?

The **Milky Way** is the galaxy we live in. On a dark night you can see part of it as a pale band of
light across the sky: the combined glow of countless stars in its disc, seen from inside.

It is a spiral galaxy with a central bar. Its disc of stars spans more than 100,000 light-years and
holds more than 100 billion stars. At its very centre sits a supermassive [black hole](doc:black-hole),
**Sgr A***, of about 4.3 million solar masses.

The Sun lies in the disc, about 8.3 kpc (27,000 light-years) from the centre. It orbits the centre at
about 247 km/s and takes roughly 200 to 230 million years for one lap.

## How do we know?

Mapping the Galaxy from inside is hard, like drawing a forest while standing among the trees. Dust in
the disc hides distant stars from visible light. Astronomers combine several methods.

**Distance to the centre.** The GRAVITY instrument at ESO's Very Large Telescope followed the star S2
on its orbit around Sgr A*. From the size and timing of the orbit it measured the distance to
the centre: R0 = 8,275 ± 9 (statistical) ± 33 (systematic) parsecs, and the black hole's mass:
4.297 million Suns.

**Spiral arms.** Radio waves pass through dust. Mark Reid and colleagues (2019) measured the
[parallaxes](doc:parallax) of about 200 masers (natural microwave lasers) in young, massive star-forming
regions with radio telescopes. Their positions suggest the Milky Way has **four main spiral arms**, and
their motions give the Sun's circular speed: about 236 km/s, plus the Sun's own extra motion
(247 km/s in total).

**Stars around us.** [Gaia](doc:gaia-mission) measured nearly 1.5 billion stellar parallaxes, mapping our
part of the disc in 3D.

## Surprising but true

- Dividing the Sun's angular speed around the centre (30.3 km/s per kpc, Reid et al. 2019) into a full
  circle gives about 203 million years per orbit; NASA quotes about 230 million. Either way the Sun,
  which formed about 4.6 billion years ago, has gone round only some twenty times.
- Young massive stars within 7 kpc of the centre form an extremely thin layer, with a scale height
  of only about 19 parsecs: that is why they define the Galactic plane so well.
- In 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope published the first image of Sgr A*: a bright ring about
  52 micro-arcseconds across.
