## What is it?

Gaia BH3 is a [black hole](doc:black-hole) that formed from a single massive star. It has about 33 times
the mass of the Sun, which makes it the most massive black hole of stellar origin known in our Galaxy.

It is "dormant": it is not pulling gas from a neighbour, so it does not shine in X-rays the way many
known black holes do. We know it is there only because an ordinary star orbits it. That star is an old
giant, a bit less massive than the Sun but almost five times larger, at a distance of about 1,900
light-years in the constellation Aquila. The star is bright enough (magnitude 11.2) to see in a modest
amateur telescope; the black hole beside it is completely invisible.

## How do we know?

ESA's [Gaia](doc:gaia-mission) satellite measures the positions of more than a billion stars again and
again over many years. While checking the data being prepared for its fourth release (DR4), the team
noticed that this star was moving in a small ellipse on the sky, 27 milliarcseconds across in its
semi-major axis, repeating every 11.6 years. That wobble is the star circling an unseen partner.

From the size, shape and tilt of the wobble, and the distance from Gaia's [parallax](doc:parallax),
astronomers worked out the true size of the orbit. Kepler's laws then give the total mass of the pair.
The star's own mass, 0.76 Suns, was found from its spectrum and brightness, and what is left is the dark
companion: 32.7 Suns. An ordinary star of that mass would be thousands of times brighter than the giant,
so the partner must be a black hole.

Gaia also measured the star's speed toward and away from us (radial velocity) over the same years. These
measurements were made independently from the positions, and they fit the same orbit, which is a strong
check. Ground-based telescopes then took detailed spectra of the star.

## Surprising but true

- The discovery was so important that the Gaia team published it in April 2024, well before the DR4
  data release, so other astronomers could start follow-up work.
- The orbit is very elongated. The star swings between about 4.4 and 28 au from the centre of mass, a
  range that in our Solar System would reach from a little inside Jupiter's orbit to nearly Neptune's.
- The star contains very little iron and other heavy elements. This supports the idea that very heavy
  stellar black holes, like those seen merging in gravitational-wave detectors, come from stars poor in
  heavy elements.
- The pair belongs to the Milky Way's halo, not its disc. Its motion and chemistry point to the ED-2
  stream, probably the remains of a star cluster torn apart by our Galaxy long ago.
- Before Gaia BH3, Gaia had found two other dormant black holes in its third data release: Gaia BH1
  and Gaia BH2. Gaia BH3 is the third, and by far the heaviest.
