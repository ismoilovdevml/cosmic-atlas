## What is it?

A **pulsar** is a rapidly spinning [neutron star](doc:neutron-star): the crushed core of a massive star,
only about 20 km across. It has a very strong magnetic field and sends out two narrow beams of radio
waves. As it spins, the beams sweep around like the light
of a lighthouse. Each time a beam crosses the Earth, our radio telescopes record a short pulse.

Pulsars spin with amazing regularity. Their pulses come at intervals from milliseconds to seconds,
and the pulses of the first one were so regular that its discoverers at first wondered whether the
signal could be artificial.

## How do we know?

The first pulsar was found by accident. In 1967 Jocelyn Bell Burnell, a graduate student at Cambridge,
noticed an odd signal in the data of a new radio telescope. Faster recordings in November 1967 showed a
train of pulses a bit more than a second apart (today's catalogue value is 1.337 s). Her supervisor
Antony Hewish nicknamed the source LGM-1, for "Little Green Men". Bell Burnell doubted it: the source
kept appearing in the same part of the sky, as a natural object would. The explanation turned out to
be a spinning neutron star. The 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Martin Ryle and to Hewish
"for his decisive role in the discovery of pulsars"; Bell Burnell was not among the winners.

Today radio telescopes time pulsars to tiny fractions of a second. The ATNF Pulsar Catalogue (version
2.9.0) lists **4,503** of them.

## Surprising but true

- The fastest known pulsar, PSR J1748−2446ad in the globular cluster Terzan 5, spins **716 times per
  second**. Its discoverers showed its radius must be under 16 km; even so, its equator moves at up to
  about a quarter of the speed of light.
- The Crab pulsar, at the heart of the [Crab Nebula](doc:crab-nebula), spins about 30 times per second.
  It was born in the supernova seen in 1054.
- Planets were found around a pulsar, PSR B1257+12, in 1992 by timing its pulses: three years before
  the first planet around a Sun-like star (see [exoplanet](doc:exoplanet)).
- The plaque on Pioneer 10 shows where the Sun is by its position relative to 14 pulsars (see
  [interstellar message](doc:interstellar-message)).

## Where to find it in this atlas

Pulsars are dead stars and very faint in visible light, so they are not in the Gaia star layer as
pulsars. The atlas's catalogue of pulsars from the ATNF list is planned; until then you can find the
places where famous pulsars live. Fly to the [Crab Nebula, M1](#focus=M1), home of the Crab pulsar
(2.0 kpc). Search for any pulsar by name, such as "PSR B1919+21" (the first pulsar) or "PSR B0833-45"
(the Vela pulsar): the search asks the CDS Sesame service. If Sesame has no usable distance, the view
turns towards the pulsar from the Sun and the card says "direction only".
