## What is it?

Neptune is the eighth and farthest planet from the Sun. It orbits about 4.5 billion km away, roughly
30 times farther than Earth. Even moving at the speed of light, sunlight needs about four hours to
get there.

Like Uranus, Neptune is an ice giant: about four times wider than Earth, with a hot, dense fluid of
"icy" materials (water, methane and ammonia) above a small rocky core. Its air is mostly hydrogen and
helium with a little methane, and the methane absorbs other colours but reflects blue. Many famous
Voyager 2 pictures show a deeper blue than the real planet, because the team boosted the contrast to
make the clouds easier to see.

Neptune is the windiest world in the Solar System: clouds of frozen methane race across it at more
than 2,000 km per hour.

## How do we know?

Neptune was the first planet found by mathematics. Astronomers noticed that Uranus did not move quite
as predicted, as if something unseen were pulling on it. Urbain Le Verrier calculated where that
unseen planet should be, and on 23 September 1846 Johann Gottfried Galle found Neptune close to the
predicted spot, on his first night of searching. John Couch Adams had made a similar prediction.

Neptune's mass comes from the orbits of its moons and from the path of Voyager 2, the only spacecraft
to have visited. Voyager 2 flew past in the summer of 1989. It photographed a storm, the
Great Dark Spot, large enough to hold the whole Earth, and measured the length of Neptune's day,
16.11 hours, from the rotation of its magnetic field.

## Surprising but true

- Galileo Galilei saw Neptune in 1612 and 1613, but recorded it as a fixed star, more than two
  centuries before it was recognised as a planet.
- One Neptune year lasts 164.8 Earth years. Neptune completed its first full orbit since its discovery
  only in 2011.
- Triton, Neptune's largest moon, orbits in the opposite direction to the planet's spin. This
  suggests that Neptune captured it rather than formed it. Triton's surface is about −235 °C, yet
  Voyager 2 saw geysers on it throwing ice more than 8 km high.
- The Great Dark Spot seen in 1989 has since disappeared, but new storms have formed elsewhere on the
  planet.
- Neptune's rings include named rings such as Galle, Le Verrier and Adams, honouring the people of the
  discovery story.
