## What is it?

Pluto is a dwarf planet: a small, round world that orbits the Sun far beyond Neptune, among the icy
bodies of the Kuiper belt. When Clyde Tombaugh discovered it on 18 February 1930, it was counted as
the ninth planet. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union (IAU) adopted a definition of a planet,
and Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet.

The reason is the third rule of that definition. A planet must orbit the Sun, be mostly round, and be
massive enough that its gravity has cleared other objects of similar size from around its orbit.
Pluto meets the first two rules but not the third: it shares its region of space with many other
icy bodies. The IAU recognises five dwarf planets so far: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris.

## How do we know?

For 85 years Pluto was just a faint point of light. That changed on 14 July 2015, when NASA's
New Horizons spacecraft flew past it and sent back the first close-up pictures. They showed a varied
world: wide plains of frozen nitrogen with no craters at all, and mountains 2 to 3 km
high made of blocks of water ice.

Pluto's mass is measured from the orbits of its moons; the largest, Charon, is about half Pluto's
size. Pluto also has a thin atmosphere, made mainly of nitrogen, with methane and carbon monoxide.

## Surprising but true

- Pluto is smaller than our Moon: its diameter is only about two-thirds of the Moon's.
- New Horizons photographed a large, bright, heart-shaped patch on Pluto's surface.
- Pluto's orbit is so stretched that at its closest it comes nearer to the Sun (4,435 million km)
  than Neptune ever does (Neptune's closest point is 4,471 million km). So for part of each orbit,
  Pluto is closer to the Sun than Neptune.
- Its orbit is also tilted by 17° to the plane in which Earth moves, much more than any planet's.
- A year on Pluto lasts 248 Earth years, so Pluto has not yet completed one orbit since 1930. That will
  happen around the year 2178.
- Pluto spins "backwards", like Venus and Uranus, and one Pluto day lasts about 6.4 Earth days.
- Surface temperatures range from about −226 °C to −240 °C.
