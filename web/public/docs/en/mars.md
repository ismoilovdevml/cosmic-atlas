## What is it?

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and the outermost of the four rocky planets. It is about half
the size of Earth and has only about a tenth of Earth's mass. Its reddish colour comes from iron
minerals in the dust and rocks that have oxidised, in other words rusted.

Mars has an atmosphere, but a very thin one: the pressure at the surface is about 6 millibars, roughly
0.6 % of the air pressure at sea level on Earth. That air is 95 % carbon dioxide. Because the air is so
thin, liquid water cannot last long on the surface today, even though the planet has water ice at its
poles and underground.

## How do we know?

Mars's mass comes from the orbits of its moons and of the spacecraft that circle it: the faster
something orbits at a given distance, the heavier the planet it orbits. Its size and shape come
from radar and laser altimeter measurements from orbit. The air pressure and temperatures were
measured on the spot by landers, starting with the Viking landers in the 1970s, and today by NASA's
rovers Curiosity and Perseverance.

The evidence for ancient water is written in the landscape. Orbiters have photographed dried-up river
valleys, river deltas and old lakebeds, and rovers have found minerals that form only in liquid
water. Some features suggest huge floods about 3.5 billion years ago.

## Surprising but true

- A day on Mars is only about 40 minutes longer than a day on Earth, and its axis is tilted almost as
  much as ours, so Mars has seasons too. Each season lasts about twice as long, because the Martian year
  is 1.88 Earth years.
- Valles Marineris, a system of canyons, is about 3,870 km long, up to 600 km wide and about 9.3 km
  deep. It is longer than the radius of Mars itself (3,390 km).
- Olympus Mons is a volcano whose base is about the size of the US state of Arizona.
- Mars's two moons, Phobos and Deimos, were discovered by Asaph Hall in 1877. They are small and
  potato-shaped, not round like our Moon.
- The distance between Earth and Mars changes a lot: from about 55 million km at the closest to about
  400 million km when Mars is on the far side of the Sun. Good alignments repeat every 780 days, about 26 months, so
  that is how often launch windows to Mars open.
