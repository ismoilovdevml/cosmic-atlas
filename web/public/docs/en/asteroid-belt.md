## What is it?

The main asteroid belt is a broad ring of rocky bodies that circle the Sun between the
orbits of Mars (1.52 au) and Jupiter (5.20 au). Asteroids are rocky leftovers from the early formation of
the Solar System. They range from small rocks up to Ceres, which is big enough to be a dwarf planet.

NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory defines a main-belt asteroid by its orbit: its average distance from
the Sun lies between 2.0 and 3.2 au, and it never comes closer to the Sun than 1.666 au. That ring is
about 1.2 au, or some 180 million km, wide.

## Key numbers

| | Main asteroid belt |
|---|---|
| Location (average orbital distance) | 2.0–3.2 au from the Sun |
| Catalogued main-belt asteroids | 1,396,520 (JPL, 13 November 2026) |
| All catalogued asteroids | 1,580,494 (JPL, 13 November 2026) |
| Estimated number larger than 1 km | 1.1–1.9 million |
| Total mass | less than the Moon's |
| Largest body | Ceres, radius 476 km (a dwarf planet) |
| Ceres's share of the belt's mass | about 25 % |
| Largest asteroid (not counting Ceres) | Vesta, about 530 km across |

## How do we know?

Asteroids are found as faint points of light that move against the background stars from night to
night. Once a body has been seen often enough, its orbit can be calculated and it receives a number in
the catalogues. The counts in the table come straight from the Small-Body Database of NASA's Jet
Propulsion Laboratory, and they keep growing as surveys report more objects.

Close-up knowledge comes from spacecraft. NASA's Dawn orbited Vesta, leaving it on 4 September 2012,
and then reached Ceres in 2015. By tracking how a spacecraft's motion changes near an asteroid,
scientists can weigh it. Such measurements show how heavy Ceres is: according to NASA, Ceres alone
holds about a quarter of the mass of the whole belt.

## Surprising but true

- More than a million asteroids sound like a crowded place, but all of them together have less mass
  than our Moon. Spread over a ring 180 million km wide, they leave enormous empty spaces between them.
- Ceres was discovered by Giuseppe Piazzi in 1801. Since 2006 it has
  been classed as a dwarf planet, together with Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris.
- Ceres may be up to 25 % water, most of it probably in an icy mantle under the surface.
- A day on Ceres lasts about 9 hours, and its year about 4.6 Earth years, at an average distance of
  2.8 au from the Sun.
- The numbers grow fast: JPL counts 1,580,494 known asteroids of all kinds, and NASA estimates that
  the belt alone has 1.1 to 1.9 million bodies larger than 1 km, plus many millions of smaller ones.
