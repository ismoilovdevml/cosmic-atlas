## What is it?

M87* (say "M87-star") is the supermassive [black hole](doc:black-hole) at the centre of M87, a giant
elliptical [galaxy](doc:galaxy) in the constellation Virgo. Its mass is enormous: about
6.5 billion times the mass of the Sun.

On 10 April 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) collaboration published the first image of a black
hole, and this was it. The picture shows a glowing, slightly lopsided ring of hot gas around a dark
centre. The dark centre is the black hole's "shadow": light that comes too close is bent around or
swallowed, so we see a hole in the glow.

The galaxy M87 itself was catalogued by Charles Messier in 1781. It is huge, with roughly 15,000
globular star clusters around it.

## Key numbers

| | M87* |
|---|---|
| Mass | (6.5 ± 0.7) billion Suns |
| Distance | 16.8 ± 0.8 Mpc (about 55 million light-years) |
| Diameter of the bright ring | 42 ± 3 micro-arcseconds |
| Event horizon radius (Schwarzschild radius) | about 19 billion km (128 au), computed from the mass |
| Mass compared with Sgr A* | about 1,500 times larger |
| Observations | 5, 6, 10 and 11 April 2017, wavelength 1.3 mm |

## How do we know?

To see something as small as a black hole's shadow from 55 million light-years away, you need a
telescope the size of the Earth. The EHT makes one by linking radio observatories far apart:
in 2017 it used eight stations at six sites around the world. All of them recorded radio waves at a
wavelength of 1.3 mm at the same time, and the recordings were combined afterwards. This method is
called very-long-baseline interferometry: the farther apart the dishes, the finer the detail.

The team made images with several independent methods, and all of them gave the same ring, stable over
four nights. Its diameter, 42 micro-arcseconds, is tiny: a micro-arcsecond is a millionth of an
arcsecond, and the full Moon is about 1,900 arcseconds wide. The team then compared the ring with a
large library of computer simulations of hot gas around a spinning black hole that follow Einstein's
general relativity. The best match gives the mass, 6.5 ± 0.7 billion Suns, if one uses the distance of 16.8 Mpc.

The ring is brighter on one side. The EHT explains this with plasma that circles the black hole at
nearly the speed of light: the side moving toward us looks brighter, like a car's headlights look
brighter when it comes toward you.

## Surprising but true

- The event horizon of M87* is about 256 au across. That is wider than the whole distance Voyager 1, our
  farthest spacecraft, has travelled since 1977 (about 172 au).
- M87* is about 1,500 times heavier than [Sgr A*](doc:sgr-a-star), the black hole at the centre of our
  own galaxy. Gas takes days to weeks to circle M87*, but only minutes to circle Sgr A*.
- The black hole itself emits no light. Everything in the famous picture is glowing gas outside the
  event horizon; the dark middle is where that light is missing.
- The radio waves in the 2019 image left M87 about 55 million years ago. We see the black hole as it
  was then, not as it is today.
- The centre of the ring is about ten times dimmer than the ring itself, which is what a shadow
  predicted by general relativity should look like.
