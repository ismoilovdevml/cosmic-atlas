## What is it?

**SETI**, the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence, tries to answer an old question: are we alone?
Instead of looking for life directly, SETI looks for **technosignatures**: signs of technology, such as
radio signals that nature is not known to make. Very narrow radio signals, squeezed into a tiny range
of frequencies, are a favourite target. But nature can be tricky: in 2024 a team found faint natural
narrowband signals from cold hydrogen clouds.

**The honest answer today: no confirmed technosignature has ever been found.** So far, candidate
signals have turned out to be human interference, or have never been seen again.

## How is it done?

The first modern search was **Project Ozma**. In April 1960 Frank Drake pointed a radio telescope at
Green Bank, West Virginia, at two nearby stars, Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani. The team heard
something once, but it came from a high-flying aircraft. Ozma was both the first SETI experiment and
the first SETI false alarm.

Since then, searches have grown enormously. **Breakthrough Listen**, announced on 20 July 2015 with
100 million dollars for 10 years, observes with the 100-metre Green Bank Telescope, the 64-metre Parkes
telescope in Australia and an optical telescope that looks for laser flashes. Its targets include the
million stars closest to Earth, the plane and centre of the Milky Way and the 100 nearest galaxies, and
its data are open to the public.

## Surprising but true

- **The Wow! signal.** On 15 August 1977 the Big Ear radio telescope of Ohio State University recorded a
  strong, narrow signal near 1420 MHz, the natural frequency of hydrogen, from the direction of the
  constellation Sagittarius. It lasted about 72 seconds, the time the sky took to drift through the
  telescope's beam. Astronomer Jerry Ehman circled it on the printout and wrote "Wow!". It has never
  been detected again, despite many searches. Its origin is **still unknown**. In 2024 one team proposed
  a natural explanation, a sudden brightening of a cold hydrogen cloud triggered by a flare from a
  magnetar; this is a hypothesis, not a settled answer.
- **blc1.** In 2019 Breakthrough Listen recorded a narrow signal while observing Proxima Centauri. A
  detailed analysis in 2021 showed it was radio interference from human equipment, not an alien
  signal.
- Even the first pulsar was briefly nicknamed "LGM-1", for "Little Green Men", before it was understood
  as a natural object (see [pulsar](doc:pulsar)).
