## What is it?

When a star like the Sun runs out of fuel, it swells into a giant and gently blows off its outer
layers, which glow for a while as a planetary [nebula](doc:nebula). What remains is the bare core: a
**white dwarf**.

A white dwarf no longer makes energy by fusion. It is simply a very hot ember that cools down over
billions of years. A new white dwarf can be hotter than 100,000 K.

White dwarfs are tiny and extremely dense. A typical one has about half the mass of the Sun packed into
a ball only a little bigger than the Earth. Its density is about 10^9 kg per cubic metre, some 200,000
times the Earth's average: a teaspoon (5 ml) would weigh about 5 tonnes. Its surface gravity is about
100,000 times the Earth's.

## How do we know?

The first famous white dwarf is Sirius B, the faint companion of [Sirius](#focus=Sirius), first seen in
1862. In 2005 the Hubble Space Telescope weighed it by measuring the *gravitational redshift* of its
light, an effect predicted by Einstein: light climbing out of strong gravity is stretched to redder
wavelengths. The result: 98 % of the Sun's mass in a ball about 12,000 km across, smaller than the
Earth.

There is a limit to how heavy a white dwarf can be: about 1.4 solar masses, the **Chandrasekhar
limit**. Above it, the pressure of its electrons can no longer hold it up.

Gaia changed the field. White dwarfs are faint for their colour, so on a diagram of brightness against
colour they form their own sequence, well apart from ordinary stars. From Gaia EDR3, Gentile Fusillo and
colleagues (2021) selected about 359,000 high-confidence white-dwarf candidates.

## Surprising but true

- The Sun will become a white dwarf too. So will the vast majority of stars.
- Old white dwarfs freeze. Gaia showed a "pile-up" of white dwarfs at a certain brightness. As their
  carbon-oxygen cores crystallise, they release heat that delays their cooling by about a billion years.
- White dwarfs can explode. The supernova that Johannes Kepler saw in 1604 was a type Ia: the
  thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf (see [supernova remnant](doc:supernova-remnant)).

## Where to find it in this atlas

White dwarfs are in the [Gaia stars](#layer=stars-gaia) layer, mixed with ordinary stars. The atlas's
star table flags a star as a likely white dwarf when its absolute G magnitude is fainter than
10 + 2.6 × (BP − RP), for colours BP − RP below 1.6: that line runs through the empty gap between
ordinary stars and the white-dwarf sequence. The rule ignores dust, so it is a guide, not a final
classification. You can spot candidates yourself: on the info card, look for a star with a small or
negative BP − RP (white or bluish) whose brightness code M is fainter than about +10. Most are very
close to the Sun, because they are so faint.

Planetary nebulae mark the moment a white dwarf is born. Look at the [Ring Nebula](#focus=M57) or the
[Helix Nebula](#focus=Helix%20Nebula): each is the glowing shell of a dying Sun-like star.
