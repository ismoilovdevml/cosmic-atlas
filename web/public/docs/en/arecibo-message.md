## What is it?

The Arecibo message is a short radio message sent into space on the afternoon of 16 November 1974,
from the giant Arecibo radio telescope in Puerto Rico. The occasion was a ceremony for the telescope's
upgrade, which included a powerful new radar transmitter. The message was transmitted once and took
about three minutes.

It was aimed at M13, the Great Cluster in Hercules, a ball of roughly 300,000 stars. The organisers
were clear about its purpose. In Cornell University's words, it was "strictly a symbolic event, to show
that we could do it". It was a demonstration of what the new transmitter and the huge dish could do,
not a serious attempt to start a conversation.

## Key numbers

| | Arecibo message |
|---|---|
| Sent | 16 November 1974 |
| Frequency | 2,380 MHz (wavelength about 12.6 cm) |
| Length | 1,679 bits (binary digits) |
| Picture size | 23 columns × 73 rows |
| Duration | about 3 minutes, sent once |
| Target | globular cluster M13 in Hercules |
| Distance to M13 | about 24,200 light-years (7.42 kpc) |
| Distance travelled by November 2026 | about 52 light-years |

## How do we know?

The message and its design were published by the staff of the National Astronomy and Ionosphere Center,
which ran Arecibo, in the journal Icarus in 1975. Frank Drake wrote it together with Richard Isaacman,
Linda May and James C. G. Walker, with help from Carl Sagan and others.

The 1,679 bits were sent by shifting the frequency of the signal up and down over a range of about
10 Hz, a method similar to the way old computer modems sent binary code over a telephone line. The
number 1,679 is the product of two prime numbers, 23 and 73, and a receiver who lays the bits out as
23 columns and 73 rows gets a picture.

That picture shows the fundamental chemicals of life, the formula for DNA, a simple drawing of a human
being, a crude diagram of the Solar System and a picture of the Arecibo telescope itself.

## Surprising but true

- The message travels at the speed of light, yet by November 2026 it has covered only about
  52 light-years, a tiny fraction of the way to M13.
- Cornell's 1999 description gives M13's distance as about 25,000 light-years; a modern measurement
  gives about 24,200. Either way the message will arrive in more than 24,000 years, and any answer
  would need just as long to come back. No reply is possible for tens of thousands of years.
- The Arecibo telescope that sent it has collapsed: its 900-ton instrument platform fell on
  1 December 2020, damaging the dish.
- No confirmed signal from extraterrestrials has ever been received, in reply to Arecibo or otherwise.
  The most famous unexplained candidate is the [Wow! signal](doc:wow-signal) of 1977, which was never
  detected again.
