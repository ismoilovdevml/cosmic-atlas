## What is it?

Zoom out far enough and galaxies stop looking like scattered dots. They form a network: long
**filaments** of galaxies, flat **walls** or sheets, dense **clusters** where filaments meet, and vast
**voids** in between with very few galaxies. Astronomers call this pattern the **cosmic web**. NASA
describes it as "the large-scale backbone of the cosmos".

Most of the web's mass is **dark matter**, which we cannot see directly but which makes up most of the
material in the universe. Ordinary gas falls along the same dark-matter filaments, and galaxies form
where the gas collects. Filaments of gas can stretch over 10 million light-years from galaxies.

## How do we know?

To see the web you need the third dimension: the distance to each galaxy. On the sky alone, galaxies at
all distances overlap. Redshift surveys measure a [redshift](doc:redshift) for each galaxy and so place
it in 3D.

In 1986 Valérie de Lapparent, Margaret Geller and John Huchra published "A slice of the universe": the
redshifts of galaxies in a thin strip of sky. Instead of a smooth spread, the slice
showed galaxies lying on the edges of large empty bubbles. Later surveys, such as the Sloan
Digital Sky Survey and now [DESI](doc:desi-survey), mapped millions of galaxies
and confirmed the pattern on ever bigger scales.

A second method uses motions. If you know a galaxy's distance independently, you can subtract the
expansion of the universe from its redshift and find how it moves through space. Galaxies flow towards
dense regions and away from voids. In 2014 Brent Tully and colleagues used such flows to outline our
home supercluster, **Laniakea** ("immense heaven" in Hawaiian): about 500 million light-years across,
with roughly 100,000 galaxies and the mass of a hundred million billion Suns.

## Surprising but true

- In one NASA-supported study, an algorithm inspired by the way slime mould grows traced the web's filaments from the positions of 37,000 SDSS galaxies. The
  light of 350 distant quasars then confirmed hydrogen gas along the predicted filaments.
- The Milky Way does not sit in a special place. It lives in a small group, the
  [Local Group](doc:local-group), which is one tiny part of Laniakea.
- When we look at the far parts of the web, we see them as they were billions of years ago (see
  [lookback time](doc:lookback-time)), so a single map also shows the web growing over time.

## Where to find it in this atlas

Fly out to about 200 Mpc with the [DESI](#layer=galaxies-desi) and [2MRS](#layer=galaxies-2mrs) layers
on. In a full 3D view, millions of galaxies at different depths overlap. To read the web, open the
Layers panel and turn on **Thin slab**: only galaxies within a thin layer through the centre of the
view are drawn, as in the classic slice maps. A half-thickness of ±25 Mpc shows filaments and voids at
about 200 Mpc; at 1 Gpc, ±75 Mpc shows the whole DESI wedge. The last stop of the **Scale tour** also
shows the cosmic web.

Nearby, the [Cosmicflows-4](#layer=galaxies-cf4) layer shows 55,877 galaxies with distances that do not
depend on redshift, the kind of data used to find Laniakea.
