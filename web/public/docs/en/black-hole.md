## What is it?

A **black hole** is a place where so much mass is packed into so small a space that its gravity lets
nothing escape, not even light. Its boundary is called the **event horizon**: anything that crosses it
can never come back out. The black hole itself cannot be seen, but its gravity affects everything near
it.

Black holes come in very different sizes:

- **Stellar-mass black holes**, a few to a few dozen times the mass of the Sun, form when the core of a
  very massive star collapses. A core heavier than about 3 solar masses cannot stop as a
  [neutron star](doc:neutron-star).
- **Supermassive black holes**, millions to billions of solar masses, sit at the centres of most large
  galaxies, including ours. When they swallow a lot of gas, they power [quasars](doc:quasar).

## How do we know?

There are several independent ways to "see" the invisible.

**Orbits.** Stars that circle an unseen mass reveal how heavy it is. The GRAVITY team followed the star
S2 around the centre of the Milky Way and measured 4.297 million solar masses for
[Sgr A*](doc:sgr-a-star). Gaia found black holes the same way, from the wobble of ordinary stars that
orbit a dark partner: **Gaia BH1** (2022), 9.6 solar masses about 480 pc away, the nearest known black
hole when it was found, and **Gaia BH3** (2024), 32.7 solar masses at 590 pc.

**Gravitational waves.** On 14 September 2015 the two LIGO detectors recorded ripples in space-time
from two black holes, of about 36 and 29 solar masses, merging about 410 Mpc away (luminosity distance). The result
was a single black hole of 62 solar masses; the missing 3 solar masses were turned into gravitational-wave energy.
It was the first direct detection of gravitational waves.

**Pictures of the shadow.** The Event Horizon Telescope links radio dishes across the Earth into one
huge telescope. In 2019 it showed a bright ring around a dark centre in the galaxy M87: the shadow of a
black hole of 6.5 billion solar masses. In 2022 it published a similar image of Sgr A*, about
4 million solar masses.

## Surprising but true

- When it was announced in 2024, Gaia BH3 was the heaviest black hole of stellar origin known in our
  Galaxy. Its companion star is old and very poor in metals, supporting the idea that metal-poor massive stars leave heavy black
  holes behind.
- The ring of Sgr A* is about 52 micro-arcseconds across, and M87*'s about 42: similar sizes on the sky,
  although M87* is about 1,500 times heavier, because it is also much farther away.
- Gaia BH1's partner is a Sun-like star on a 186-day orbit. The team concluded there is "no plausible
  astrophysical scenario" for that orbit without a black hole.
