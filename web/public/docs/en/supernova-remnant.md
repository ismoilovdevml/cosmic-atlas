## What is it?

A **supernova** is the explosion of a star at the end of its life. What is left afterwards is a **supernova remnant**: an expanding cloud of hot gas, sweeping up
the thin gas between the stars.

There are two main ways to make one:

- A **massive star**, about 8 solar masses or more, runs out of fuel. Its core collapses into a
  [neutron star](doc:neutron-star) or a [black hole](doc:black-hole), and the outer layers are blasted
  away.
- A **white dwarf** in a pair of stars takes matter from its companion until it nears about
  1.4 solar masses, then blows itself apart in a thermonuclear explosion (a type Ia supernova).

Supernovae spread heavy elements made inside stars through the Galaxy, heat the gas between the stars
and accelerate cosmic rays.

## How do we know?

Remnants glow in many kinds of light, from radio waves to X-rays and gamma rays given off by the hot
ejected gas. Astronomers collect them in catalogues: David Green's catalogue of Galactic supernova
remnants, kept at the Mullard Radio Astronomy Observatory in Cambridge, lists **310** of them
(2024 version).

For a few remnants we know the exact year of the explosion because people saw it. Chinese astronomers
recorded a "new star" on 4 July 1054, bright enough to see in daylight for several weeks. Its remnant
is the **Crab Nebula** (M1), about 6,500 light-years away. At its centre spins a pulsar, the
collapsed core, 30 times per second. In 1604 Johannes Kepler and many others watched another
supernova; Chandra X-ray images show its remnant, about 13,000 light-years away, and it was a type Ia
explosion of a white dwarf.

## Surprising but true

- The star that exploded as the Crab supernova had about ten times the Sun's mass.
- The Crab's gas filaments are still flying apart at over 3 million miles per hour (more than
  1,300 km/s), nearly a thousand years after the explosion.
- Green's catalogue grew by 21 remnants since its 2019 version, and 5 old entries were removed because
  they turned out to be clouds of gas lit by hot stars (HII regions), not remnants. Identifying a
  remnant is not always easy.

## Where to find it in this atlas

Supernova remnants from the Messier and NGC lists are searchable. Fly to the
[Crab Nebula, M1](#focus=M1) ([article](doc:crab-nebula)); the atlas's catalogue places it at
2.0 kpc, about 6,500 light-years. The [Veil Nebula, NGC 6960](#focus=NGC%206960) is listed as a
supernova remnant too, but the catalogue has no distance for it, so the view only turns towards it.

Explosions leave compact objects behind. Read about [pulsars](doc:pulsar) and
[neutron stars](doc:neutron-star) to see what the Crab's core became.
