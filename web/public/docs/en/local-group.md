## What is it?

The **Local Group** is the small family of galaxies that includes our own. Two big spiral galaxies
dominate it: the [Milky Way](doc:milky-way) and the **Andromeda Galaxy** (M31). The next most massive
members are the **Triangulum Galaxy** (M33) and the Large Magellanic Cloud. Around them swarm many dwarf galaxies: the Large and Small Magellanic
Clouds, bright enough (magnitudes 0.4 and 2.2) to see with the naked eye from the southern sky, and dozens of faint
"satellites" that orbit the Milky Way and Andromeda.

Alan McConnachie's catalogue lists more than 100 galaxies with distance estimates placing them within
3 Mpc of the Sun, from the satellites of the two big spirals to lonely dwarfs on the outskirts. Beyond
that lie the next groups of galaxies.

## How do we know?

Local Group galaxies are close enough for astronomers to see their individual stars. That allows
distance methods based on stars whose true brightness is known, such as pulsating Cepheid variables,
which Edwin Hubble used in 1923 to show that Andromeda lies far outside the Milky Way.

The best distances are now very precise. In 2019 a team measured the distance to the **Large
Magellanic Cloud** using 20 eclipsing binary stars, pairs of stars that pass in front of each other:
49.59 kpc, precise to about 1 %. That distance is a key step on the ladder used to measure the
expansion of the universe.

## Surprising but true

- Andromeda and the Milky Way are often said to be on a collision course, merging in about five
  billion years. A 2024 study using Gaia and Hubble data found it is far less certain: including the
  pull of M33 and the Large Magellanic Cloud, there is a probability of close to 50 % that the two
  galaxies do **not** merge in the next 10 billion years.
- The atlas's catalogue puts the LMC at 50.6 kpc, with a 16–84 % range of 48.5–52.7 kpc. The 1 %
  measurement (49.59 kpc) falls inside that range: a good example of
  [distance uncertainty](doc:distance-uncertainty). Its light set out about 160,000 years ago.
- Many Local Group dwarfs are named after the constellation they appear in: Draco, Fornax, Sculptor,
  Carina.
